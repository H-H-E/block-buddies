# Mentor Pedagogy: Advanced Teaching Skills

> This section provides Teen Mentors with the soft skills required to be an effective teacher.

---

## Core Principle

> **The goal is not to show off how much the Mentor knows, but to facilitate the Mentee's discovery.**

The best teachers ask questions, not give answers.

---

## The Art of the Question (Socratic Method)

Instead of giving answers, Mentors should use **"Guiding Questions"** to lead Mentees to the solution.

### Bad vs Good Responses

| Bad Mentor Response | Good Mentor Response (Socratic) | Purpose |
|--------------------|--------------------------------|---------|
| "You put the file in the wrong folder." | "Where does the game look for mods? Does it look on the Desktop or somewhere else?" | Forces recall of the file path concept |
| "Here, let me fix that code for you." | "The game is giving a syntax error. It says 'Unexpected Token'. What does a JSON list usually end with?" | Encourages syntax analysis |
| "That won't work." | "What do you think will happen if we run it like this? Let's try it and see." | Encourages hypothesis testing |
| "You need Fabric API." | "The error says it's missing something. What does it say it's looking for?" | Teaches log reading |
| "Your RAM is too low." | "The game froze and said 'Out of Memory.' What do you think 'memory' means for a computer?" | Builds conceptual understanding |

---

## The Wait Time Rule

After asking a question, **count to 5 in your head** before speaking again.

**Why?**
- Thinking takes time
- Silence feels uncomfortable, but it's productive
- Mentees often know the answer but need time to formulate it

> If you answer your own question, the Mentee learns nothing.

---

## The "I Don't Know" Response

When a Mentee says "I don't know," they often mean:
1. "I haven't thought about it yet"
2. "I'm afraid of being wrong"
3. "I genuinely don't know"

**Responses for each:**

| Situation | Response |
|-----------|----------|
| "I haven't thought about it" | "Take your time. What's your best guess?" |
| "Afraid of being wrong" | "There's no wrong answer here. What's your instinct?" |
| "Genuinely don't know" | "That's okay! Let's figure it out together. Where would we look for this information?" |

## Managing Frustration (Emotional Regulation)

Coding is frustrating. Kids will rage-quit. The Mentor's job is to **normalize failure**.

### Recognize the Signs

| Signal | What It Means |
|--------|---------------|
| Going silent | Overwhelmed, needs a break |
| Rapid clicking/typing | Frustrated, not thinking carefully |
| "This is stupid/boring" | Defeated, needs a win |
| Changing the subject | Avoiding the problem |
| Looking away from screen | Disengaged |

---

### The Reset Toolkit

| Technique | When to Use | Script |
|-----------|-------------|--------|
| **The Break** | When frustration is high | *"Let's pause the coding for a sec. Tell me about what you built in Minecraft this week."* |
| **The Win Button** | When they need a confidence boost | *"You know what, let me show you this one shortcut..."* (Give them an easy win) |
| **The Reframe** | When they think they failed | *"You didn't break it—you discovered what doesn't work. That's exactly what programmers do."* |
| **The Story** | When they feel alone | *"The first time I tried this, I crashed my game 5 times. Everyone does."* |

## The Rubber Duck Method

Teach the Mentee to explain their problem to an inanimate object (or the Mentor) **line by line**.

**Why It Works:**
- The act of explaining forces organized thinking
- Often, the Mentee will discover the solution mid-explanation
- It's a professional debugging technique used by real programmers

**Script:**
> *"Okay, pretend I'm a rubber duck who knows nothing about computers. Explain what you're trying to do, step by step."*

## The 5-Minute Rule

Calibrate struggle time based on Tech Fluency:

| Tech Level | Struggle Time Before Intervention | Rationale |
|------------|-----------------------------------|-----------|
| **Level 0** | 30 seconds | Prevent disengagement |
| **Level 1** | 2 minutes | Let them try, but don't let them spiral |
| **Level 2** | 5 minutes | Build resilience; they can handle it |

**The Goal**: Just enough frustration to learn, not so much they give up.

---

## Scaffolding Levels

Adjust your support dynamically based on what the Mentee needs in the moment.

### Level 1: Modelling ("I do, you watch")

**When**: Complete confusion, first time seeing a concept.

**How**: Share your screen. Narrate every action.

> *"Watch what I do. I'm opening File Explorer. Now I'm typing %appdata% in the address bar. See how I press Enter?"*

---

### Level 2: Guided Practice ("We do together")

**When**: They understand the concept but aren't confident yet.

**How**: Let them drive, but provide prompts.

> *"Okay, your turn. Where do we start? ... Good, you opened File Explorer. Now what do we type?"*

---

### Level 3: Fading ("You do, I watch")

**When**: They've done it successfully at least once.

**How**: Hands off. Let them struggle a bit.

> *"Show me how to install this mod. I'll just watch."*

### Level 4: Independence ("You do alone")

**When**: Ready for a challenge.

**How**: Give a task, step away (metaphorically).

> *"Your homework: install this mod before our next session. Send me a screenshot when it works."*

## Celebration & Recognition

Small wins matter enormously for young learners.

### Micro-Celebrations

| Moment | Celebration |
|--------|-------------|
| First successful mod install | *"YES! You did it! You are officially a modder now!"* |
| Fixed their own bug | *"You figured that out yourself—that's huge!"* |
| Explained something correctly | *"Perfect explanation. You could teach this."* |
| Asked a great question | *"That's exactly the kind of question engineers ask."* |

### Avoid

- Empty praise ("Good job" with no specifics)
- Praising only outcomes (also praise effort and process)
- Comparing to others ("Most kids can't do this")

---

## When You Don't Know the Answer

It's okay not to know everything. Model good behavior:

**Script:**
> *"Great question—I actually don't know that. Let's figure it out together. Where should we look?"*

**Benefits:**
- Shows that not knowing is normal
- Models the research process
- Demonstrates lifelong learning attitude

---

## Communication Do's and Don'ts

### DO:
- ✅ Use encouraging language
- ✅ Celebrate small wins
- ✅ Ask questions to check understanding
- ✅ Be patient with repeated questions
- ✅ Use screen sharing to demonstrate
- ✅ Admit when you don't know something

### DON'T:
- ❌ Share your personal contact info
- ❌ Ask about family/personal situations
- ❌ Make promises you can't keep
- ❌ Continue if you're frustrated (take a break)
- ❌ Talk down to them
- ❌ Take over their mouse/keyboard (let them do it)

---

## The Mentor Mindset Checklist

Before each session, remind yourself:

- [ ] I am here to guide, not to impress
- [ ] Struggle is learning, not failure
- [ ] Questions are better than answers
- [ ] Their pace, not mine
- [ ] Celebrate the small stuff
- [ ] It's okay if things break (that's the lesson!)

## Handling Difficult Situations

### "This is boring"

**Translation**: The challenge level is wrong (too hard OR too easy).

**Response**: Switch activities. Ask what they'd rather build.

---

### "Can we just play Minecraft?"

**Translation**: Need a break from structured learning.

**Response**: *"Let's do 10 more minutes on this, and then we can explore. Deal?"*

Or, pivot to in-game teaching (commands, building, etc.).

---

### They're Way Behind/Ahead

**Behind**: Slow down. Revisit Core Trunk. It's okay to spend extra sessions.

**Ahead**: Skip ahead or add a challenge. *"Okay, now do it without my help."*

---

### Technical Difficulties Eating the Session

**Reality**: Sometimes tech just doesn't work. That's okay.

**Script**: *"Tech issues happen to everyone. We learned something today about troubleshooting. Next week we'll pick up where we left off."*

Document what went wrong, ask for help before next session.

---

## Mentor Self-Reflection

After each session, ask yourself:

1. What went well?
2. What was challenging?
3. What could I do better next time?
4. Did the Mentee seem engaged?
5. Do I need help or resources for next session?
