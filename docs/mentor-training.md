# Block Buddies Mentor Training

*For teen mentors aged 14-18 running the beginner pilot with children around age seven.*

This training prepares you to run your first session safely and confidently. Read it fully before your first session; revisit sections before each new module.

## 1. Your Job in One Sentence

Make Minecraft feel fun and doable for a kid who looks up to you - by following their lead, celebrating their ideas, and never taking over.

## 2. Building Rapport Without Acting Fake

Kids can tell when you're pretending. Be yourself, but be the patient version of yourself.

**Do:**
- Show real enthusiasm for THEIR ideas ("Whoa, a trapdoor elevator? Tell me how you thought of that!")
- Share honest bits about your own play ("I still get lost in caves sometimes")
- Remember details they told you last week and bring them up

**Don't:**
- Force slang or baby talk
- Pretend to love things you don't
- Talk down to them or over-praise every tiny thing

## 3. Talking With Seven-Year-Olds

- Ask ONE question at a time.
- Give them a few seconds of silence to think - silence is thinking, not failure.
- Use concrete choices: not "what do you want to do?" but "house or garden?"
- Narrate what YOU see them doing: "Oh I see you're putting the door on the roof - interesting choice!"

## 4. Offering Bounded Choices

Two or three options is magic. More than that overwhelms; zero bores them.

Script:
> "Okay builder - today we've got three choices: a tall tower, a wide cottage, or something underground. Which one is calling to you?"

If they say "I don't know":
> "No worries - want me to pick two and you pick one?"

## 5. Demonstrating Without Taking Over

The child does EVERY interesting thing themselves. You may demonstrate ONE small action, slowly, while narrating. Then hand control straight back.

Script:
> "Watch me do ONE step... okay see how I placed the block there? Your turn - same spot on your screen."

Never:
- Take their device/mouse unless they physically cannot act AND they ask
- Build more than one block past demonstrating
- Fix their build without asking first

## 6. Using Short Video Segments

Only use approved segments from the media registry. Rules:

1. Pre-watch the exact segment before the session.
2. Play it via YOUR screen-share, never on their device.
3. Pause at each pause point and ask what they noticed.
4. Keep segments under 5 minutes before they act again.
5. If video won't load: switch instantly to live demo. Don't apologize twice - just move on.

## 7. Prompting Prediction & Observation

Before any attempt:
> "What do you think will happen if you press that?"

After:
> "Was that what you expected? Why do you think it did that?"

This builds thinking habits WITHOUT turning into a quiz. If they shrug - fine, move on.

## 8. Handling Silence, Distraction, Frustration, Overexcitement

| Situation | What's happening | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Quiet for 30+ seconds | Thinking OR stuck OR tired | "Hey, what's going on in builder-brain right now?" |
| Wandering off-topic | Normal! Relationship IS the point | Listen 30 seconds, then bridge back: "...okay wait, but should we show your creeper that thing?" |
| Frustrated ("I CAN'T do it!") | Task mismatch | Drop difficulty immediately: "Let's do the easy version - you press, I'll find blocks" |
| Crying/real distress | Stop activity | "Want to take a break? We can just walk around." Tell coordinator after. |
| Overexcited chaos | Fun! But unfocused | Channel it: "YES okay - big energy - let's use ALL of it on this one wall" |

## 9. Giving One Hint at a Time

Hint ladder rule: start vague, get specific ONLY as needed. Never give away the answer while a hint would work.

Example (child stuck placing blocks):
1. "What are you trying to put there?"
2. "Check your hotbar - which slot has it?"
3. "Tap that number/slot, THEN tap where you want it."
4. Only then: model one placement.

## 10. Managing a Shared Build

In shared worlds, the child owns decisions. You may:
- Gather materials when asked
- Build a section THEY assigned ("you do the roof")
- Recover broken states

You may not:
- Redesign anything
- Finish "the fun part"
- Build faster than they asked

## 11. Protecting Their Creative Ownership

Praise choices, not just results: "I love THAT you made the door purple" beats "good job."

If they ask "is it good?":
> "It's YOURS. That's what makes it good. What's YOUR favorite part?"

## 12. Technical vs Emotional Difficulty

Ask yourself: "Is this hard because the game is confusing, or because they're having a feeling?"

- Technical → hint ladder, demo, fallback mini-win
- Emotional → pause, empathize, offer low-stakes activity ("want to just pet the animals for a bit?")

Both are fine. Neither means failure.

## 13. Communicating With Guardians

Guardians may be nearby during sessions. Greet them by name. After sessions, send a brief guardian-visible summary (the module's parent summary template gives you the format):

> "Today Maya built her dream house called Rainbow Castle. She designed all of it herself and added a secret room. She wants to decorate inside next time!"

Keep it warm, specific, and short. Never diagnose or complain in these notes.

## 14. Documenting Sessions Briefly

After each session, log in under 3 minutes:
- What they built/did today
- What they chose next
- One moment of pride you saw
- Anything the coordinator should know

That's it. Not an essay.

## 15. Escalation & Safeguarding Boundaries

Report to coordinator IMMEDIATELY if:
- The child mentions harm to themselves or others
- Anyone pressures you to move communication outside approved channels
- A guardian asks for something that feels unsafe or inappropriate
- You feel uncomfortable for ANY reason - no explanation needed

You never promise secrecy. Script:
> "That sounds really serious. I care about you being safe, so I'm going to talk to [coordinator] about it - they know how to help."

Never exchange personal socials, numbers, or DMs. Even kind ones. Even if asked sweetly.

## 16. When You Don't Know the Answer

Say so honestly:
> "Honestly? Not sure! Want to figure it out together?"

Then experiment like equals. This teaches the BEST lesson: not knowing is the start of finding out.
